Final-year project: serrated wingtip devices

University of Bath · Final Year Project · 2026 – 2027

Context

Wingtip vortices cause a large share of an airliner's drag and set how far apart aircraft must fly at airports. Winglets reduce this, and studies show that serrated or wavy edges can weaken the vortex further, but their effect on lift and drag is unsettled and no experiment has compared the leading, top and trailing edges on the same winglet. In my self-initiated final-year project at the University of Bath, I am testing an A350-style wingtip device with sine-wave serrations on each edge in an open-jet wind tunnel, measuring lift and drag and visualising the vortex with smoke.

What I did

  • Came up with the project and wrote the student-led proposal, inspired by the serrated fins of humpback whales.
  • Reviewed the literature on wingtip devices and serrated edges, finding conflicting force results and no wind tunnel study of trailing-edge serrations on a winglet.
  • Wrote a parametric Python tool that generates the wingtip outline with plain or slanted sine-wave serrations, with the slanted humps lined up with the airflow.
  • Reshaped the method with my supervisor: an experiment-first study, leading edge first, using a rigid wing section with swappable tips.
  • Planning the tunnel tests: a force balance I will calibrate myself, smoke visualisation and repeat runs for uncertainty, with Ansys CFD as optional comparison.
  • Keeping a decision log and risk register so every change of scope has a recorded reason.

Geometry tool

My Python script draws the side view of the wingtip device from a few inputs: bottom edge length, trailing-edge angle, height, top edge length and an optional fillet at the top corner. It adds sine-wave serrations to the top and trailing edges, either plain or slanted so that each hump lines up with the airflow, and fits a whole number of waves plus a half-wave on each edge. The serrations are centred on the original edge, so the device's area stays about the same between configurations and does not explain differences in lift and drag.

Decisions so far

I defined the serrations by amplitude and wavelength: two continuous variables that make trends easy to see, and the closest published study found wavelength mattered more than waveform shape. I measure the vortex and the forces as separate outcomes, because a weaker vortex does not automatically mean less drag. Every tip fits the same rigid wing section, so differences come from the tip, not the set-up. With my supervisor I chose the open-jet tunnel for easy tip swaps and smoke access, an experiment-only core method because it guarantees real results, and a test order of leading edge, then top edge, then trailing edge, each tested on its own before any combinations.
